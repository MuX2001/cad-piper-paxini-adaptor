# Mount concept comparison — preliminary

The design must leave the stock Piper finger intact and expose the tactile face to the grasp gap. The supplied gripper geometry is a mesh reference rather than a dimensioned finger drawing, and no stock screw interface has been verified.

| Concept | Retention and contact load | Build and assembly | Fit risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Two-piece bolted clamshell over the distal finger | Captures the finger without adhesive or drilling; a closed distal end can resist sliding. A separate plate can fasten the sensor through its intended mounting holes. | Two FDM pieces and common screws; adjustable cavity and clearance. | Needs a physical fit check on the tapered finger and a confirmed sensor hole pattern. |
| One-piece slip-on sleeve with end cap | End cap resists sliding toward the tip, but retention in the other direction depends on friction or a snap feature. | Simplest print; may need flexible material or a tight fit. | High risk of loosening, damage during installation, or poor tolerance across printers. |
| Bracket using an existing finger feature | Would give a direct fastener load path if a suitable tapped interface exists. | Potentially fewer printed parts. | No suitable feature is verified in the supplied finger mesh; cannot be selected on evidence now. |

The bolted clamshell is the strongest preliminary choice. It uses the finger as a captured reference, allows replacement after a failed fit test, and keeps all modifications in printed parts. Four side bolts can tighten the two halves without passing through the gripper; removable compliant liners take up the modeled fit clearance. The sensor interface uses its manufacturer mounting holes, keeps the sensing face clear, and routes the cable proximally to an external tie lug. The housing must not become the primary contact surface.

The material load path on each finger is sensor mounting screws → printed inner plate → clamshell walls and distal stop → original finger. The supplier uses the same finger mesh on each jaw with mirrored URDF placement, so two copies of the clamp are posed on opposite fingers. The virtual assembly confirms opposed active-face normals; both cable routes still need separate physical fit checks. Exact grip force, stiffness, and slip resistance cannot be verified from these assets; no FEA or physical test has been performed. A fit-test print and a controlled low-force grasp are required before use.

For a first-pass load check, the supplied M2324 manual lists 25 N normal and ±10 N tangential measurement ranges (PDF page 5). If load were shared evenly by six sensor screws, those values would average 4.2 N normal and 1.7 N tangential per screw. Actual edge contact can concentrate force in fewer screws. The 2.6 mm printed inner plate spans a roughly 32 mm finger cavity and has a central cable opening, so local plate flex, layer-direction strength, liner creep, and screw preload are the main unverified risks. These averages are not a strength rating or safety factor; test deflection and slip at low force before approaching the sensor's measurement limit.

As an illustrative stiffness calculation only, treating a 24 mm-wide, 2.6 mm-thick section as a simply supported 32.4 mm beam under a central 25 N point load gives about **7.5 MPa nominal bending stress** and **0.34 mm deflection** if the effective printed modulus is assumed to be 1.5 GPa. The real part has a cable opening, screw holes, complex support, and print-direction effects, so this uniform-beam estimate can understate flex and local stress. It is not a rated load or FEA result.
